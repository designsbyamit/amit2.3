An essay on influence

The Business Seat

On design, influence, and the quiet politics of consequential decisions.

The difference between being invited into a business conversation and changing what the business decides.

The meeting has been going on for forty minutes when someone asks the designer what the interface should look like.

Until that moment, the conversation has concerned the cost of delay, the engineering effort required to ship the next release, the demands of a strategic customer, and the revenue forecast for the quarter. Product has explained the roadmap. Engineering has described the constraints. Finance has put a number against the risk.

Now it is the designer's turn.

The designer speaks about clarity, consistency, the user's mental model. The room listens. Someone nods. A decision is made, and the meeting moves on.

Nothing has gone wrong, exactly. The designer has been consulted. The work will probably be good.

And yet something important has happened.

The designer has been invited to shape the interface after others have shaped the decision that produced it.

This is how professional boundaries survive inside organisations. Not through hostility, nor even through deliberate exclusion, but through habits so reasonable that nobody thinks to question them. Engineering builds. Product prioritises. Business decides. Design makes the result comprehensible.

Each discipline performs its assigned role with increasing sophistication. The arrangement becomes more efficient, and therefore harder to challenge.

The problem is not that design lacks influence over the interface. The problem is that the interface may be the least consequential part of the decision.

01 / The paradoxThe strange economy of expertise

Organisations are remarkably good at rewarding people for becoming indispensable within a narrow territory.

The engineer who can solve an impossible technical problem earns trust. The product manager who can negotiate a roadmap across competing interests earns authority. The designer who can make a complicated system feel effortless earns admiration.

But admiration is not the same as influence.

Expertise creates a paradox. The better you become at performing a particular function, the more confidently an organisation assigns you work belonging to that function. Your excellence becomes the argument for keeping you where you are.

A designer who repeatedly rescues difficult interfaces may find themselves invited to more interface reviews, not more product strategy meetings. The reward for being exceptionally good at solving the problem is a larger supply of the same problem.

Competence can become a very comfortable cage.

Craft matters. Without it, design becomes a collection of opinions dressed in attractive slides. But craft, by itself, does not determine which problems deserve to exist, which investments are worth making, or which compromises a company should refuse.

Those questions belong to a different order of work.

Consider a product that users find confusing. A designer might simplify its navigation, improve its information hierarchy, and reduce the number of steps required to complete a task. All worthwhile interventions.

But suppose the confusion comes from a product strategy that has accumulated years of exceptions, acquired features, incompatible workflows, and competing business priorities.

The interface is not the source of the problem. It is where the problem becomes visible.

Polishing the interface without questioning the structure is like improving the signage in a building whose corridors lead nowhere.

The designer's real contribution may begin when they stop asking how to make the existing system easier to use and start asking why the system has become so difficult to use in the first place.

That question is more dangerous. It crosses boundaries. It challenges decisions made elsewhere. It may require a designer to understand the economics of a feature, the cost of maintaining an architectural choice, or the political bargain that produced a particular workflow.

It also creates an opportunity that craft alone rarely offers: the opportunity to change the decision itself.

02 / A personal incidentThe decision behind the deliverable

I encountered a version of this problem early in my leadership journey, when I began building a design team in 2014.

The immediate need was relatively clear. We had client requirements to fulfil and positions to fill. It would have been easy to approach hiring as a matching exercise: identify the work, find the people who could do it, and get the team moving.

But I began to recognise a larger question. We weren't simply filling roles. We were deciding what kind of team we wanted to become.

The skills required to deliver the next assignment were not necessarily the skills that would make the team stronger in the years ahead. Hiring only for the work immediately in front of us risked turning today's requirements into tomorrow's limitations.

So the problem changed. Rather than looking only at the positions we needed to fill, I began considering the experiences of both candidates and the hiring team, and how the hiring process itself could be redesigned.

The distinction may seem small, but it changes the nature of the work. Instead of optimising a process within an existing frame, we were questioning the frame.

A hiring decision is also a design decision. It shapes capabilities, relationships, the distribution of expertise, and the choices an organisation will be able to make in the future. The consequences extend well beyond the moment a candidate accepts an offer.

This is where design's influence can become consequential: in recognising that the thing being designed is sometimes not an interface or a service, but the conditions under which an organisation will operate.

The same principle applies to product decisions. A designer who can improve a screen adds value to a product. A designer who can recognise that the product is solving the wrong problem may change its direction.

The latter requires a different kind of fluency.

03 / LegitimacyThe seat is a political object

A table is a simple piece of furniture. In an organisation, it is a map of legitimacy.

Who is invited to a meeting tells us whose judgement is expected to matter. Who speaks first tells us whose framing is likely to survive. Who presents the recommendation, who owns the budget, and who makes the final call reveal the difference between participation and authority.

Designers sometimes speak of earning a seat at the table as though the table were a neutral institution, waiting patiently for the deserving to arrive.

It is not.

The table has a history. It has established loyalties, familiar vocabularies, and rules about what counts as evidence. It may welcome a designer who speaks fluently about customer needs but remain unconvinced when that designer challenges a commercial assumption. It may applaud a workshop and ignore the decision that the workshop was supposed to change.

The invitation is real. So are its limits.

This is why simply placing designers inside business meetings is insufficient. Proximity creates the possibility of influence; it does not guarantee it.

A designer can attend every strategic review and still be treated as the person who makes decisions presentable after everyone else has made them.

The more difficult task is to become useful at the point where the problem is still unsettled.

Understand the business model, not just the product. Learn where revenue comes from, what threatens it, how the company allocates resources, and which customer behaviours determine success. Understand engineering well enough to distinguish a genuine constraint from an inherited assumption. Learn to recognise when a disagreement about an interface is actually a disagreement about ownership, incentives, or strategy.

And then do something with that understanding.

Bring the right people together before their positions harden. Make the consequences of competing choices visible. Reframe a debate that has become trapped in departmental interests. Offer a direction that acknowledges the constraints without mistaking them for destiny.

Sometimes this means defending a design principle. Sometimes it means abandoning a preferred solution because the evidence points elsewhere. Sometimes it means arguing that the organisation is solving the wrong problem altogether.

The purpose is not to win every argument.

It is to improve the quality of the decisions the organisation can make.

That is a more demanding ambition than securing a place in the meeting. It also makes the seat less important.

You begin to discover that influence does not always require the authority to decide. It requires the ability to change what others consider before they do.

04 / The difficult transitionThe designer must become uncomfortable

There is a reason this transition can be difficult even for experienced designers.

Design offers a form of psychological shelter. It provides methods, artefacts, a body of knowledge, and a community that recognises its own standards. Within this territory, competence is legible. A designer can point to a prototype, a research finding, a system of components, or a resolved interaction and explain what has improved.

Business leadership is less tidy.

The evidence is incomplete. Stakeholders disagree. Decisions have consequences that cannot be fully predicted. The best option may be the one that creates the least damaging compromise rather than the one that achieves an ideal experience. Success may depend on persuading people who do not report to you, do not share your vocabulary, and do not necessarily agree that the problem deserves attention.

There is no perfect prototype for this kind of work.

The designer must learn to operate without the reassurance of a clearly bounded problem. They must become comfortable making judgements across disciplines, exposing uncertainty rather than hiding it behind polished presentations, and taking responsibility for outcomes that cannot be attributed to design alone.

This last point deserves attention.

Designers sometimes claim ownership of success when an experience improves, then retreat into disciplinary boundaries when a product misses its targets. But the business does not experience product decisions as a collection of independent functions. Customers encounter one product. Organisations bear one set of consequences.

To participate meaningfully in that reality, design must accept some of the ambiguity that comes with shared responsibility.

This does not mean designers should become product managers, finance leaders, or engineers in disguise. An organisation does not become stronger when every discipline dissolves into a generic form of management.

It becomes stronger when different forms of expertise can shape a common decision without losing what makes each one distinct.

The designer's contribution is not to think exactly like the business. It is to bring a way of seeing that the business would otherwise lack, while understanding enough of its realities to make that perspective consequential.

The tension must remain.

Without design, a business may optimise what it can measure and overlook what it cannot yet see. Without business understanding, design may defend an experience that cannot be delivered, sustained, or justified. Neither perspective is sufficient by itself.

Their disagreement is not a problem to eliminate. It is often where the most valuable thinking begins.

05 / The deeper ambitionWhat happens when design is no longer in the room?

There is a final irony in the pursuit of influence.

The designer who wants to be indispensable may spend years trying to secure a permanent invitation to every important conversation. Yet the more mature ambition is not to make every decision depend on a designer's presence.

It is to help build an organisation in which design thinking has become part of how decisions are made.

Product managers begin questioning the quality of a workflow before a designer raises it. Engineers consider the implications of an architectural choice for the coherence of the experience. Business leaders recognise that a short-term gain may create a long-term burden for customers. Teams learn to distinguish a feature request from the problem that produced it.

None of this happens because a designer has delivered a presentation about the value of design.

It happens because people have experienced better decisions and begun to adopt the habits that produced them.

This is a quieter form of leadership. It offers less visible credit. The designer may not be named when a team avoids an unnecessary feature, resolves a cross-product inconsistency, or chooses a more durable direction. The work becomes part of the organisation's judgement rather than remaining an identifiable design intervention.

There is a professional risk in this. Invisible influence can become invisible labour. Organisations may absorb the thinking while continuing to undervalue the people who helped introduce it. Designers should not romanticise obscurity or confuse a lack of recognition with evidence of impact.

But there is a distinction between being unseen because your contribution is overlooked and becoming less necessary because the organisation has learned something from your presence.

The first is a failure of recognition.

The second may be a measure of leadership.

Perhaps this is the real test of design's place in business: not whether a designer is invited to the table, nor even whether their recommendations are accepted, but whether the organisation begins to see differently because they were there.

When the next consequential decision is made, will the business need to invite design into the room, or will the way it thinks about the problem already bear the mark of design?
