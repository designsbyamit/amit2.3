Why Is Collaboration So Hard When Everyone Is Right?

Have you seen the problem beneath the problem?

A story about design, engineering, competing convictions, and the leadership challenge of getting capable people to stop defending their answers and start solving the problem together.

A few years ago, I was asked to design a marketplace for DevOps professionals, a place where they could discover plugins and extensions to deploy applications to the cloud.

On the surface, it looked like a straightforward design problem. Underneath, it carried a history of disagreement.

A couple of months earlier, engineering had explored a solution of its own. Our senior design director had explored one too. The two approaches reflected fundamentally different views of the product. Engineering believed the design wasn't scalable. Design leadership felt the engineering-led solution compromised the experience.

Both sides had reasons to believe they were right. And that was precisely the problem.

When capable people disagree, we tend to assume that better evidence, a stronger argument, or a more convincing solution will settle the matter. But sometimes the disagreement has less to do with the quality of the ideas than with the people, responsibilities and histories attached to them.

We weren't just comparing two solutions. We were looking at the problem through two different sets of convictions.

If we couldn't find common ground, the engineering solution would move forward, and design would lose its influence over the product. I realised that my job wasn't simply to design a better marketplace. It was to help the teams discover a better way to solve the problem together.

01 / ReframingThe problem beneath the problem

Before designing anything, I did my homework. I spoke to each stakeholder, trying to understand not just what they wanted, but why they wanted it.

What made engineering concerned about scalability? What constraints was product working within? What did design believe was missing from the experience?

Those conversations changed how I saw the problem.

Engineering wasn't the enemy. Neither was design leadership. Each was protecting something important, but neither perspective was sufficient on its own.

This is one of the less obvious challenges of working across disciplines. People rarely arrive at a disagreement believing they are defending something unreasonable. They have responsibilities to fulfil, expertise to protect, and outcomes for which they will be held accountable. Their positions are often rational within the boundaries of their own worlds.

The difficulty begins when those boundaries become invisible to the people standing inside them.

A debate about scalability versus usability can quickly become a debate about whose judgement should prevail. Professional conviction hardens into ownership. Ownership becomes territory. And the original problem, the product itself, quietly slips out of focus.

The team is still working hard. Everyone is still trying to do the right thing. Yet progress becomes difficult because each side is defending its answer rather than examining the question.

I began to see that we needed to do more than reconcile two solutions. We needed to create a shared understanding of the problem we were trying to solve.

02 / The interventionChanging the conversation

So I brought design, engineering and product into one workshop.

The intention wasn't to convince everyone that design had the right answer. It was to give everyone the same context and a shared understanding of the problem before we began exploring solutions.

We looked at different approaches, examined what worked across them, and discussed the trade-offs. We prioritised ideas against value, effort, time and complexity. Each perspective had a place in the discussion, but no perspective was allowed to stand in for the whole problem.

The conversation began to shift.

Instead of asking which team's solution should prevail, we started asking what the product actually needed. Instead of treating scalability and experience as competing ideals, we examined how our choices would affect both. Instead of defending individual proposals, we looked for the principles that should guide the final direction.

Nobody had to be convinced that the final solution was the design solution. We arrived at it together.

Once we had agreed on the principles and trade-offs, I designed the interface around them. The result was a marketplace for DevOps plugins and extensions that the teams found genuinely useful.

But the interface wasn't the most consequential outcome.

The more important change was in how people approached design afterwards. They became more willing to involve design early because they had experienced a different kind of contribution from us. We weren't simply advocating for a better UI. We were helping the team solve the larger product problem while accounting for the realities of engineering, product and business.

We hadn't won an argument. We'd changed the conditions under which the argument could be resolved.

A practical framework

The leadership move is not to choose a side faster. It is to create the conditions for a better decision.

03 / The paradoxWhen conviction becomes a constraint

There is a peculiar paradox in professional expertise: the very thing that makes people valuable can also make collaboration difficult.

The engineer who understands the system's limitations may be right to challenge a design that cannot scale. The designer who understands the user's needs may be right to challenge a technically elegant but confusing solution. Both may have excellent reasons for their positions.

But being right about a part of the problem does not necessarily mean seeing the whole of it.

This is where professional egos become interesting. Ego isn't always arrogance or the need to dominate a conversation. Sometimes it is more subtle: the difficulty of letting go of an idea we have invested our judgement in, particularly when that idea represents our expertise or the standards we have spent years defending.

The more capable we are, the easier it becomes to mistake the boundaries of our knowledge for the boundaries of the problem.

The answer isn't to eliminate conviction. Organisations need people who care deeply about the quality of their work and are willing to defend important principles. Nor is the answer to force consensus, where everyone compromises until nobody objects and the solution becomes mediocre.

The real work is to create enough shared understanding that people can question their own assumptions without feeling that they have lost.

That requires more than good intentions. It requires a deliberate effort to understand other people's constraints, make trade-offs explicit, and establish a purpose that is larger than any one team's preferred solution.

In our case, that purpose was the product.

Once the teams could evaluate their ideas against a shared understanding of what the product needed, disagreement became useful. It helped us examine the consequences of our choices rather than defend the legitimacy of our positions.

The differences didn't disappear. They became productive.

04 / The leadership moveChange the conditions, not the people

Looking back, I don't think the most important thing I did was facilitate a successful workshop. It was recognising that the problem we needed to solve had changed.

Initially, the task appeared to be designing a marketplace. Then it became clear that we also needed to address the way design, engineering and product were approaching the decision. The design challenge had become a collaboration and change-management challenge.

That shift changed my role.

Instead of arriving with a solution and trying to secure agreement, I took responsibility for creating the conditions in which the teams could develop one together. Instead of treating alignment as something to achieve after the work, we made it part of the work itself.

This is a leadership lesson I continue to carry with me: sometimes the most valuable contribution is not to provide the answer, but to recognise what is preventing a group of capable people from finding one.

It requires listening before proposing, understanding before judging, and being willing to question the framing of a problem rather than simply becoming more efficient at solving it.

It also requires a degree of restraint. When we are convinced that our solution is better, it can be difficult to step back and make room for a collective answer. Yet leadership is not measured by how often our ideas prevail. It is measured, in part, by whether the people around us can produce better outcomes because of how we work together.

05 / What remainsFour principles I carry forward

The experience reinforced four principles that continue to shape how I approach collaboration and design leadership.

01

Understand constraints before proposing solutions.

Ask every function not only what it wants, but why. The reasoning behind a position often reveals more useful opportunities than the position itself.

02

Co-create the answer.

When people help shape a solution, they understand the trade-offs behind it. Shared ownership is more durable than agreement secured through persuasion.

03

Speak the language of the ecosystem around you.

Understand engineering's constraints, product's priorities and the business problem. Not to imitate other disciplines, but to connect their perspectives and help the organisation see the whole.

04

Be the initiator.

Don't wait for someone else to resolve the tension. Take responsibility for improving the conditions in which decisions are made, and for the product outcomes connected to them.

These principles sound simple. Practising them is not. It requires people to move beyond the comfort of their own expertise, work through disagreements they cannot resolve with a prototype, and accept that good outcomes often depend on perspectives they do not personally own.

I still think about how differently the marketplace project could have unfolded. We might have chosen the engineering-led solution. Design might have continued to argue for a better experience. Both teams could have walked away believing they had been right all along.

Instead, we found a way to move forward together.

Perhaps that is the real challenge of collaboration. Not to make everyone agree, nor to persuade everyone to see the world through the same lens, but to create a space where different ways of seeing can contribute to something none of us could have produced alone.

Because sometimes the greatest obstacle to solving a problem is not that nobody knows the answer. It is that everyone is too busy defending theirs.
